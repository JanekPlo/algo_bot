# Microstructure: slippage and funding for perpetual futures

A backtest that fills at the bar's Close and never pays to hold a position is telling a comfortable lie. On Binance USDT-M perpetual futures the gap between that idealised run and a realistic one is large enough to turn an apparent edge into a loss. This note explains the three costs a taker strategy actually pays, why the numbers land where they do, how `algo_bot` models them, and how to read the raw-versus-adjusted metrics that the model produces. The implementation lives in [`algo_bot.microstructure`](../reference/modules/microstructure.md); the decision record is [ADR-011](../adr/011-microstructure-adjustments.md).

## The three costs

A market (taker) order that opens and later closes a perpetual position pays three distinct things, and it helps to keep them separate because they have different causes and different sizes.

The first is the **exchange fee** (commission). It is a published, deterministic charge — on Binance USDT-M the standard taker fee is on the order of 0.04–0.05 % per side, so roughly 8 bps round-trip. This is the one cost the backtest engine already models: it lives in `backtesting.py`'s `commission` parameter (the project baseline is `0.0004`). Because it has always been in the engine, we treat the fee as part of the **"raw"** result rather than as a microstructure overlay.

The second is **slippage**. This is not a fee anyone charges; it is the difference between the price the backtest assumes and the price you actually get. A market order does not fill at the mid-price — it crosses the spread (you buy at the ask, sell at the bid) and, if it is large relative to the book, walks through several price levels (market impact). There is also the small drift between the instant a signal fires and the instant the order fills. For the most liquid contracts — BTC and ETH USDT-M perpetuals — the top-of-book spread is on the order of a single tick, well under one basis point, so a modestly sized taker order pays roughly half a spread plus negligible impact: about 0.5–2 bps per side. That is why the default `slip_bps` is **1.0** (≈ 2 bps round-trip). Combined with the ~8 bps round-trip fee, total transaction cost is the ~10 bps envelope the Phase 2 ROADMAP refers to. Slippage grows with size and with illiquidity, so the value is a configurable knob (`--slip_bps`), and a conservative stress run at 5 bps/side is one flag away.

The third is **funding**, and it is the cost that is unique to perpetual futures and easy to forget. A perpetual contract has no expiry, so to keep its price tethered to spot the exchange runs a periodic cash transfer between longs and shorts called funding. On Binance it settles every 8 hours, at 00:00, 08:00 and 16:00 UTC. The amount is `Notional × Funding Rate`, where notional is the mark price times your position size. When the funding rate is positive (the perpetual trades above spot, i.e. longs are crowded), **longs pay shorts**; when negative, shorts pay longs. The rate has a small fixed interest component — about 0.01 % per 8 h, which is the synthetic default in this codebase — plus a variable premium that tracks how far the perpetual has drifted from the index, and it is capped at ±0.75 % per 8 h. Two properties make funding matter for a trend strategy: it compounds over a multi-day hold (three settlements a day), and it has fat tails — during the Luna collapse or the FTX implosion, funding spiked far beyond the interest component, which is exactly the kind of stress Phase 2 needs to see.

One subtlety governs *when* funding is charged: you pay or receive **only if you hold the position at the exact settlement instant**. A position opened five minutes after 08:00 skips that settlement; a position closed five minutes before 16:00 skips that one too. This codebase honours that rule by charging funding per settlement event rather than as a smooth per-bar accrual. The boundary convention is half-open, `(entry, exit]`: a trade opening exactly at a settlement timestamp does not pay it (the fill lands just after the snapshot), while a trade closing exactly at a settlement does (it held into the snapshot). This edge is not academic — on 1-hour and 4-hour timeframes the bar timestamps coincide with 00/08/16 UTC, so trades frequently start or end exactly on a settlement.

## How the model works

The mechanics are deliberately simple: run the engine **once**, then overlay the costs on the result. The engine produces a raw equity curve (with the fee already inside it) and a list of trades. The microstructure layer walks that trade list, computes the slippage debit at each entry and exit and the funding flow at each settlement the position was open for, and subtracts the accumulated cost from the raw equity curve to produce an adjusted equity curve. The same costs are subtracted from each trade's PnL to produce an adjusted per-trade PnL.

The important design choice is that the overlay acts on the **equity curve**, not only on the trade list. The metrics that decide whether a strategy is worth taking live — Sharpe, maximum drawdown, Calmar — are computed from the equity curve, not from the trade PnL. A layer that only adjusted trade PnL (the cosmetic helper this work removed) could change the profit factor but would leave Sharpe and drawdown untouched, which would defeat the purpose. By rebuilding the equity curve we get an honest post-cost Sharpe.

There is one acknowledged approximation. The adjusted equity at any time is the raw equity minus the costs accumulated up to that time — a parallel downward shift that grows as costs pile up. True compounding would be slightly harsher, because the capital removed by early costs would itself have stopped earning. At the scale of these costs (tens of basis points) the difference is second-order, and the raw equity is always preserved on disk for anyone who wants to check by hand. The mark price used to value funding notional is likewise approximated by the bar's Close at the settlement instant, since the exchange's own mark is not stored.

## Reading raw versus post

Every run writes two metric summaries to `summary.json`: `_metrics_summary_raw` (the engine result, fee included) and `_metrics_summary_post_microstructure` (raw minus slippage minus funding). The point of keeping both is the spread between them. If a strategy shows a raw Sharpe of 1.5 and a post-microstructure Sharpe of 1.3, costs are a modest tax and the edge is real. If the same raw 1.5 collapses to 0.6 once costs are charged, the strategy was living on the fills the backtest invented for it, and it does not belong on live capital — that is precisely the judgement the Phase 2 MVP go/no-go (Session 8) has to make, and the raw-versus-post spread is the evidence. A large gap is also diagnostic in itself: it usually means the strategy trades too often (slippage dominates) or holds through adverse funding (funding dominates), and the per-trade breakdown columns in `trades.csv` (`slip_cost_quote`, `funding_cost_quote`) say which.

In the sweep, the `index.csv` carries both `sharpe_raw` and `sharpe_post`; rank on `sharpe_post`, because that is the edge you would actually receive. In walk-forward, the per-fold metrics and the stitched out-of-sample curve are post-microstructure when the layer is enabled, so the MVP pass/fail booleans already reflect realistic costs. The master switch `--microstructure none` reproduces the old raw-only behaviour for comparison or for quick iteration where costs are not yet the question.

## Defaults, and where to push them

Every default has a reason rather than a round-number convenience. `slip_bps = 1.0` comes from the sub-basis-point top-of-book spread of liquid BTC/ETH perpetuals at the size bghtrend trades. `funding_rate_synthetic = 0.0001` is Binance's fixed interest component, the floor the variable premium oscillates around, so a synthetic run is anchored to something real rather than to zero. The funding source defaults to `historical` with a synthetic fallback, so the pipeline runs before the history has been fetched (with a warning) and uses real Luna/FTX funding tails once `algo-fetch-funding` has been run. None of these is sacred: slippage in particular should be stress-tested upward for less-liquid regimes or larger size, and the raw-versus-post diagnostic exists precisely so that the sensitivity is visible rather than assumed.
